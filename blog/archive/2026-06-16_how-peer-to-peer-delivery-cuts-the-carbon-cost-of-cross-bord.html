The Hidden Emissions Problem in Business Shipping

Freight transport is responsible for approximately 11% of global CO₂ emissions, and as cross-border e-commerce and B2B trade volumes continue to grow, that figure shows little sign of reversing. For companies shipping internationally, the environmental cost of logistics is no longer a peripheral concern — it is increasingly scrutinised by procurement teams, sustainability auditors, ESG reporting frameworks, and customers who want to understand the footprint behind their supply chain.

Yet despite the urgency, most businesses face a familiar and frustrating trade-off: fast delivery means air freight, and air freight means a significant carbon burden. Slower, greener options often sacrifice reliability or introduce unacceptable lead times. The market has been slow to offer a credible third path.

Why Traditional Courier Models Are Inefficient by Design

The root of the problem lies in how conventional courier networks operate. When a business books a dedicated courier — whether by van, truck, or air — it is, in effect, commissioning a vehicle to move specifically because of that parcel. The emissions generated on that journey are directly attributable to the shipment. Even when carriers consolidate loads, the underlying logic remains the same: demand creates trips, and trips create emissions.

For Scope 3 emissions reporting under frameworks such as the GHG Protocol, these logistics emissions are increasingly difficult to discount or offset credibly. Businesses are being asked to reduce them — not simply account for them.

A Different Model: Moving Parcels on Existing Journeys

BootHop is built on a structurally different premise. Rather than dispatching a dedicated vehicle, BootHop connects businesses with verified travellers who are already making the relevant journey — whether that's a flight between cities, a drive across a border, or a regular commute along a trade corridor.
